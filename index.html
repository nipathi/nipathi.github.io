Case study · Quantum machine learning

Ten-year heart disease risk with a hybrid quantum model

We built a variational quantum convolutional neural network (VQCNN) and tested it fairly against four classical models. The quantum model did not win. The result is still useful, and this page shows exactly what happened.

Type
Final year project and research paper, B.E. 2025 to 26
Team
Muhammed Ashal PT, Awaiz Khan and me
My part
I built the quantum model and compared it with the classical baselines
Stack
Python, PennyLane, PyTorch, scikit-learn, Flask

The problem

Ten-year cardiovascular risk depends on many factors that interact in non-linear ways. Classical models can miss those interactions. We asked a simple question: can quantum feature encoding represent patient data better than classical models do?

What I built

  1. DataFramingham Heart Study and a cardiovascular Kaggle dataset, combined
  2. PreprocessingOutlier clipping, one-hot encoding, scaling, SMOTE for class imbalance
  3. Quantum encodingAngle encoding on 4 to 8 simulated qubits, CNOT entanglement
  4. Variational circuitTrainable rotations, gradients by the parameter-shift rule
  5. Classical layersConvolutional layers on the quantum output
  6. Risk scoreServed through a Flask interface

Data was split 70% train, 15% validation and 15% test with stratification. All models used identical splits. The circuits ran on a simulator (PennyLane default.qubit), not real quantum hardware.

Outcome

  • The quantum model ranked last on ROC-AUC, but sat within about 0.05 of the best classical model.
  • The paper reports it used far fewer parameters than the classical neural network.
  • We attribute the gap to the limits of simulating only 8 qubits with shallow circuits, and we say this as a hypothesis, not a proven cause.
  • Training converged steadily, which shows gradient-based learning works in a hybrid quantum and classical loop.

What I took from it

A negative result reported clearly is worth more than a flattering number. It taught me to benchmark against strong baselines first and to state the limits of an experiment.

Case study · Edge AI

Brinjal leaf disease detection on a camera chip

Many farms have weak or no internet, so sending every leaf photo to the cloud is not an option. The model had to run on a small camera board, right where the plant is.

Type
Hardware and machine learning project, Oct to Nov 2025
My part
The model: training, compression and evaluation
Hardware
Hardware build and integration by Alan John
Stack
Python, TensorFlow and TensorFlow Lite, ESP32-CAM, LoRa radio

The problem

Detect disease in brinjal (eggplant) leaves from a photo, on a device that costs a few dollars, with no internet. The model must be small, quick and accurate enough to trust.

What I built

  1. Leaf photoESP32-CAM board captures a 64 by 64 pixel input
  2. Small modelMobileNetV2, width 0.35, quantised to 8-bit integers (INT8)
  3. 7 classesHealthy, leaf spot, insect pest, wilt, mosaic virus, small leaf, white mold
  4. AlertSent over LoRa, a long-range low-power radio. No cloud.

Dataset: 14,751 brinjal leaf images across 7 classes.

Outcome

  • My first small convolutional network scored about 14%, which is chance level for 7 classes. I treated that as a failed experiment, not a result.
  • Switching to MobileNetV2 at width 0.35 and quantising to INT8 reached 84% accuracy in a 0.67 MB model.
  • The classifier was designed to run fully offline, with alerts leaving the device by radio.
  • Honest note: on-device speed and final deployed size are being re-measured, so I do not quote them here.

What I took from it

Making a model smaller is mostly about deciding what you can live without. The first model failed loudly, which was better than a number that looked fine and was wrong.
